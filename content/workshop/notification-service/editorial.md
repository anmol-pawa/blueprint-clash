# Editorial: Notification service

## Requirements
Functional: `addTemplate(id, text)` stores text with `{name}` placeholders; `addChannel(name, channel)` registers a caller-supplied channel; `send(request)` renders the template for one recipient and delivers it over one named channel. Refusals come first and in a fixed order (empty recipient, unknown template, unknown channel, missing param) and never reach a channel. Delivery calls the channel up to `maxAttempts` times: `Transient` is retried after 100, 200, 400... ms, `Permanent` stops at once, there is no wait after the last attempt, and every send starts its backoff again. The caller gets a `Receipt` with the status, the number of channel calls, the channel that delivered (or was last tried) and the body. Hidden until asked: the placeholder grammar (what is and is not a placeholder, no rescanning of values), the check order, attempts versus retries, and the rule that a registered name is never replaced. Non-goals: threads, exceptions (compiled without them), queues, preferences, provider receipts.

**Change Storm 1, fallback chain:** `makeFallbackService`: `sendVia` takes an ordered list of channels. Unregistered names are skipped, repeats are tried once, nothing registered is `UnknownChannel`; each channel gets a full retry run with its own backoff and a channel that gives up hands over at once; one body for all; the receipt totals the calls and names the channel that delivered or the last one tried. Revealable by asking whether another channel can be tried when one keeps failing.

**Change Storm 2, idempotency keys:** `makeIdempotentService`: `sendOnce(key, request)` delivers at most once per non-empty key and answers a repeat with `duplicate = true` and the original receipt. Only a delivery uses up a key; an empty key opts out. Revealable by asking about accidental repeats.

## Reference design
- `TemplateRenderer` owns the placeholder grammar. One pass over the template; a value is appended and never looked at again. It can fail (a missing param), which is why the facade checks the result.
- `TemplateStore` and `ChannelRegistry` own the two named collections and their registration rules. `ChannelRegistry::route` turns a list of names into the channels that exist, in order, without repeats.
- `RetryPolicy` is the retry rules for **one** channel: what is retried, how many calls, the doubling wait, no wait after the last attempt. The delay is a local of one run, so the policy has no mutable state and can be shared by every send.
- `Courier` carries one finished text along a route: each hop gets a whole retry run, the first delivery ends the trip, and it totals the attempts into the `Receipt`. A single-channel send is a route of one hop, so there is no second code path.
- `ServiceImpl` is the facade: validate in the documented order, ask the collaborators, return the receipt. It has no rule of its own.

**Storm 1 in the reference:** the route already existed (the base design is a route of one), so the storm adds `sendVia` as one more way to build the same route. `Courier`, `RetryPolicy`, the renderer and the stores are untouched. Be honest about what a candidate who hard-coded a single channel must change: the facade, the loop and the receipt.

**Storm 2 in the reference:** one new class, `IdempotentServiceImpl`, a Decorator that wraps a `FallbackService`, forwards everything and adds the key check in `sendOnce`. No existing class is edited. The decorator needs the wrapped service's `Receipt` to decide whether to record, which is why `Receipt` carries a status.

## Alternatives
- **A `switch` on channel type** (email, SMS, push) is the first draft most people write and it is the thing the brief warns about: every new channel edits the central function, providers cannot be faked, and the retry code ends up inside each case.
- **Retry inside each channel adapter** puts the same loop in every provider class. It does let a provider tune its own retries, but the policy (what counts as transient, how long to wait) is then in N places and the fallback chain cannot see the total effort. A shared policy with per-channel overrides is the compromise; the base answer keeps one.
- **Retry as a decorator on `Channel`** (`RetryingChannel` wraps any channel) is a respectable alternative: the service sees one `send` that already retries. It composes well, but the backoff waits and the attempt counts must be reported back out for the receipt, which means widening the interface. A policy object that runs a channel keeps `Channel` minimal.
- **Rendering with regular expressions** (`std::regex` replace over the template) is short and wrong in three ways: it is slow, it makes rescanning of inserted values an easy mistake, and the grammar lives in a pattern string nobody reads. A small hand-written scanner is longer and has a testable grammar.
- **Idempotency inside the service** (a key parameter on every call and a map in the facade) works and puts a second responsibility in the class that is already coordinating three others. A decorator keeps the base path untouched and lets callers who do not care skip it. The honest cost is one more object and four forwarding methods.
- **Recording the key before sending** (reserve first) prevents two concurrent calls from both sending, and is what a threaded design needs, but then the failure path must release the key. In a single thread, recording after the delivery is simpler and equivalent; the follow-ups cover the reservation.

## Trade-offs
Doubling without jitter is the easiest to test and the easiest to get wrong in production: many callers that fail together retry together. Capping and randomising are the first change, and they touch only `RetryPolicy`. Counting attempts per channel gives each fallback a fair chance and makes the worst case predictable (channels times attempts, plus the waits), at the price of a slow total when everything is down; a shared budget or a deadline bounds that, but the tests here deliberately pin the per-channel rule. Treating `Transient` and `Permanent` as a channel's answer pushes error-code knowledge into adapters, where it belongs, and costs one more enum value. Failing a whole send on a missing parameter is strict: a half-filled message is worse than none, but a more forgiving system might default the value; that is a product decision, so state it. The idempotency key being global to the service keeps the contract simple and puts the burden of uniqueness on the caller; scoping it to the recipient would be safer against collisions and is a two-line change in the decorator.

## What breaks at 10x
At 10x the volume the synchronous loop is the problem: a slow provider holds the caller for the whole retry run (seconds of waits). Accept the request, put it on a durable queue, and run `Courier` in workers; the receipt becomes a status. At 10x the channels, the route needs a circuit breaker per provider so a dead one is skipped immediately rather than burning attempts, plus health-based ordering. At 10x the teams, the template store becomes a managed service (versions, review, localisation) and the placeholder grammar becomes an API that cannot change casually. At 10x the callers, the idempotency table needs a time-to-live, an in-flight marker for concurrent duplicates and a shared store; the in-memory map is the first thing to outgrow.

## Level signals
- **SDE2:** a working service with channels behind an interface, templates filled from a map, a retry loop with a limit, and a receipt; handles a missing parameter when prompted; the wait and the attempt count are right after a hint; adds the fallback list with a loop.
- **Senior:** asks which failures are retryable and who decides; separates rendering, the retry rules, the registry and the route into small classes; makes the delay a local of one run and no wait after the last attempt; treats user data as text, never as template; builds the fallback so each channel has its own budget; adds idempotency as a decorator and records only successes; names the thread-safety and jitter questions.
- **Staff:** states the delivery contract (at-least-once, with deduplication to approximate exactly-once) and what each layer guarantees; designs the move to a queue with priorities, quotas, circuit breakers and a dead-letter store; separates the policy decision (which channels, when) from delivery; considers failure of the idempotency store itself and what the safe default is; talks about observability and cost per channel.
